# Mental Wellness for Older Adults

Mental health is just as important as physical health, especially as we age. Many older adults in Singapore experience loneliness, anxiety, or depression, but these conditions often go unrecognised and untreated. This guide helps you understand common mental health challenges in later life and offers practical strategies for maintaining emotional wellbeing.

## Why Mental Wellness Matters in Later Life

Growing older brings many changes that can affect how you feel. Retirement, health problems, loss of loved ones, reduced mobility, and changes in living arrangements can all take a toll on mental wellbeing.

In Singapore, studies estimate that about 1 in 5 older adults experience significant symptoms of depression, though many do not seek help. This may be because they see sadness as a normal part of ageing, feel embarrassed, or do not know where to turn.

Mental health problems are not a normal part of ageing. They are real conditions that can be treated, and seeking help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

Poor mental health also affects physical health. Depression is linked to:
- Increased risk of falls
- Faster progression of frailty
- Poorer recovery from illness and surgery
- Reduced motivation for exercise and self-care
- Greater pain perception
- Weakened immune system

## Recognising Depression in Older Adults

Depression in older adults can look different from depression in younger people. Instead of feeling sad, you might experience:

- Loss of interest in activities you used to enjoy
- Persistent tiredness and low energy, even after rest
- Difficulty sleeping or sleeping too much
- Changes in appetite or weight
- Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- Feeling worthless, hopeless, or like a burden to others
- Unexplained aches and pains
- Irritability or restlessness
- Withdrawing from family and friends

If you have been experiencing several of these symptoms for more than two weeks, please talk to your doctor. Depression is treatable, and most people improve significantly with the right support.

**Screening at polyclinics:**
During your regular health screenings at the polyclinic, your doctor may use a simple questionnaire to check for depression. Be honest in your answers. Early detection leads to better outcomes.

## Practical Strategies for Mental Wellness

### Stay Socially Connected

Social isolation is one of the strongest risk factors for depression and cognitive decline in older adults. Humans are social beings at every age.

- **Stay in regular contact** with family and friends, whether in person, by phone, or video call.
- **Join a group activity** such as exercise classes, choir, gardening, or cooking at your community club or senior activity centre.
- **Volunteer your time and skills.** Helping others gives a strong sense of purpose. Many organisations welcome older volunteers.
- **Consider joining a peer support group** if you are dealing with a specific challenge like caregiving, bereavement, or chronic illness.
- **Visit your neighbourhood senior activity centre (SAC).** There are over 100 SACs across Singapore offering daily activities, meals, and companionship.

### Stay Physically Active

Exercise is one of the most powerful natural antidepressants. It releases endorphins (feel-good chemicals) in the brain and improves sleep, energy, and self-confidence.

- Aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate activity most days.
- Walking, Tai Chi, swimming, and gentle yoga are all excellent options.
- Group exercise is especially beneficial because it combines physical activity with social interaction.
- Even on days when you do not feel like it, a short 10-minute walk can improve your mood.

### Practice Mindfulness and Relaxation

Mindfulness means paying attention to the present moment without judgement. It has been shown to reduce anxiety, improve mood, and help with chronic pain.

**Simple mindfulness exercises:**

**Breathing exercise (5 minutes):**
Sit comfortably with your feet flat on the floor. Close your eyes or look down. Breathe in slowly through your nose for 4 counts. Hold for 2 counts. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for 6 counts. Repeat for 5 minutes. Focus on the sensation of breathing. When your mind wanders, gently bring it back to your breath.

**Body scan (10 minutes):**
Sit or lie down comfortably. Starting from your toes, slowly move your attention up through your body, noticing any sensations of tension, warmth, or discomfort. Do not try to change anything. Just notice. Move through your feet, legs, hips, stomach, chest, hands, arms, shoulders, neck, and head.

**Gratitude practice:**
Each evening, think of three things from the day that you are grateful for. They can be simple things: a good meal, a phone call from a friend, sunshine on your face. This practice shifts your focus from what is wrong to what is good in your life.

### Maintain a Routine

Having a regular daily routine provides structure and purpose. Include:
- A consistent wake-up and sleep time
- Regular mealtimes
- Planned activities or outings
- Time for rest and relaxation
- Time for exercise

### Pursue Meaningful Activities

Engaging in activities that give you a sense of purpose and accomplishment is important for mental wellness.
- Learn something new: a language, a craft, or a skill
- Tend a garden, even a small balcony garden with herbs or flowers
- Read, do puzzles, or play games that challenge your mind
- Share your knowledge and life experience with younger people
- Stay involved in religious or spiritual practices if they are meaningful to you

### Limit Alcohol

While a small amount of alcohol may seem relaxing, regular or heavy drinking can worsen depression, interfere with medications, increase fall risk, and disrupt sleep. If you drink, keep it to no more than one standard drink per day.

## When to Seek Professional Help

Do not wait for things to get better on their own. Seek help if:
- You feel persistently sad, anxious, or empty for more than two weeks
- You have lost interest in most activities
- You are having thoughts of harming yourself or that life is not worth living
- You are using alcohol or medication to cope with your feelings
- Your symptoms are interfering with your daily life

**Where to get help in Singapore:**

- **Your polyclinic doctor:** Your first point of contact. They can assess your mental health, provide treatment, and refer you to specialists if needed.
- **Institute of Mental Health (IMH):** The national mental health hospital. Helpline: 6389 2222 (24 hours).
- **Singapore Association for Mental Health (SAMH):** Offers counselling and support services. Helpline: 1800-283-7019.
- **Samaritans of Singapore (SOS):** For anyone in emotional distress. Helpline: 1-767 (24 hours).
- **Silver Ribbon:** Focuses on mental health awareness and support.
- **Agency for Integrated Care (AIC) Silver Line:** 1800-650-6060 for community care support.
- **Community Mental Health teams:** Your polyclinic can refer you to community-based mental health services closer to home.

## Supporting a Loved One

If you are concerned about an older family member or friend:
- Express your concern gently and without judgement
- Listen without trying to fix everything
- Encourage them to see their doctor
- Help them stay connected and active
- Be patient; recovery takes time
- Take care of your own mental health too

Mental wellness is not about being happy all the time. It is about having the resilience to cope with life's challenges and the support to get through difficult times. Taking care of your mind is one of the most important things you can do for your overall health.
