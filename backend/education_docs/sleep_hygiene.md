# Better Sleep for Older Adults

Sleep problems are common among older adults, but poor sleep is not an inevitable part of ageing. Good sleep is essential for physical recovery, mental sharpness, mood, and immune function. This guide covers why sleep changes with age, practical steps to improve your sleep, and an introduction to Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), the recommended first-line treatment for chronic sleep problems.

## How Sleep Changes with Age

As we get older, sleep patterns naturally change:
- You may feel sleepy earlier in the evening and wake earlier in the morning.
- Sleep becomes lighter, with less time spent in deep sleep.
- You may wake more often during the night.
- Total sleep time may decrease slightly.

These changes are normal. However, persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or feeling unrefreshed after sleep is not normal and should be addressed.

**Common causes of poor sleep in older adults:**
- Pain from arthritis or other conditions
- Needing to use the toilet frequently at night (nocturia)
- Sleep apnoea (pauses in breathing during sleep)
- Restless legs syndrome (uncomfortable sensations in the legs at night)
- Anxiety and depression
- Medications that affect sleep (some blood pressure pills, steroids, certain antidepressants)
- Caffeine, especially in the afternoon or evening
- Daytime napping that is too long or too late
- Poor sleep environment (noise, light, temperature)

## Sleep Hygiene: Your Foundation for Better Sleep

Sleep hygiene refers to habits and practices that promote good sleep. While they may seem simple, consistently following these guidelines can make a significant difference.

### Keep a Regular Schedule

- Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, including weekends.
- Choose a wake-up time that you can stick to consistently.
- If you did not sleep well, still get up at your usual time. This helps reset your body clock.
- Avoid sleeping in to "make up" for lost sleep, as this can worsen insomnia over time.

### Create a Good Sleep Environment

- **Darkness:** Use blackout curtains or an eye mask. In HDB flats, even corridor lighting can seep into bedrooms.
- **Quiet:** Use earplugs or a white noise machine if noise is a problem. A standing fan can provide gentle background noise.
- **Cool temperature:** Set your air-conditioning to around 24 to 26 degrees Celsius, or use a fan. Singapore's heat and humidity can disrupt sleep.
- **Comfortable bed:** A supportive mattress and pillow are important. If your mattress is more than 8 to 10 years old, consider replacing it.
- **Bed is for sleep:** Do not watch television, use your phone, or eat in bed. This helps your brain associate the bed with sleep.

### Watch What You Consume

- **Caffeine:** Avoid coffee, tea (including Chinese tea and teh), and cola after 2pm. Caffeine can stay in your body for 6 to 8 hours.
- **Alcohol:** While alcohol may help you fall asleep, it disrupts sleep quality in the second half of the night. Avoid it within 3 hours of bedtime.
- **Heavy meals:** Do not eat a large meal within 2 to 3 hours of bedtime. A light snack is fine if you are hungry.
- **Fluids:** Reduce fluid intake in the 2 hours before bed to minimise night-time toilet trips.

### Wind Down Before Bed

- Start a relaxing routine 30 to 60 minutes before bedtime.
- Activities that promote relaxation: listening to calm music, reading a book (not on a bright screen), gentle stretching, deep breathing, or a warm shower.
- Avoid exciting television programmes, distressing news, or heated discussions before bed.
- Dim the lights in your home in the evening. Bright light signals your brain to stay awake.

### Manage Daytime Naps

- If you nap, keep it to 20 to 30 minutes maximum.
- Nap before 3pm. Later naps can interfere with night-time sleep.
- If you are having trouble sleeping at night, try reducing or eliminating naps to build up sleep pressure.

### Stay Active During the Day

- Regular physical activity improves sleep quality.
- Aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate exercise most days.
- Avoid vigorous exercise within 3 hours of bedtime, as it can be stimulating.
- Morning or afternoon exercise is ideal for promoting good sleep.

## CBT-I: The Gold Standard Treatment for Insomnia

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended by medical guidelines worldwide as the first-choice treatment for chronic insomnia, before sleeping pills. It is safe, has no side effects, and produces lasting results.

CBT-I addresses the thoughts and behaviours that keep insomnia going. It typically involves 4 to 8 sessions with a trained therapist, but even applying the basic principles yourself can help.

### Key CBT-I Techniques

**Sleep Restriction:**
This may sound counterintuitive, but spending less time in bed can improve sleep quality. If you are lying in bed for 9 hours but only sleeping for 6, you are spending 3 hours awake and frustrated.

How to apply it:
1. Calculate your average total sleep time (not time in bed).
2. Set your time in bed to match your actual sleep time (but no less than 5 hours).
3. Keep a fixed wake time.
4. As your sleep efficiency improves (you fall asleep faster and wake less), gradually increase time in bed by 15 to 30 minutes.

**Stimulus Control:**
This re-establishes the connection between your bed and sleep:
- Only go to bed when you feel sleepy (not just tired).
- If you cannot fall asleep within about 20 minutes, get up and go to another room. Do something quiet and non-stimulating (read in dim light, listen to calm music). Return to bed only when you feel sleepy again.
- Repeat this as many times as needed during the night.

**Cognitive Restructuring:**
Challenge unhelpful thoughts about sleep:
- Instead of "I must get 8 hours or I cannot function," try "Even if I sleep less tonight, I can get through tomorrow and my sleep will improve."
- Instead of "My insomnia will never get better," try "Sleep problems are treatable, and I am working on improving my habits."
- Worrying about sleep makes it harder to sleep. Remind yourself that one bad night is not dangerous.

**Relaxation Training:**
- Progressive muscle relaxation: Tense and then release each muscle group, starting from your toes and working up.
- Deep breathing: Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, breathe out for 6. Repeat for 5 to 10 minutes.
- Body scan meditation: Slowly focus attention on each part of your body, noticing sensations without trying to change them.

## When Sleeping Pills Are Not the Answer

Many older adults use sleeping pills, but they come with significant risks:
- Increased fall risk, especially when getting up at night
- Daytime drowsiness and impaired thinking
- Dependency, meaning you need higher doses over time
- Rebound insomnia when you stop taking them

If you are currently taking sleeping pills and want to reduce or stop, talk to your doctor. Stopping suddenly can cause withdrawal symptoms. A gradual tapering plan, combined with CBT-I techniques, is the safest approach.

## When to See a Doctor

Consult your doctor if:
- Sleep problems persist for more than a month despite following sleep hygiene guidelines
- You snore loudly, gasp, or stop breathing during sleep (signs of sleep apnoea)
- You have restless or uncomfortable legs at night
- You feel excessively sleepy during the day
- You are using alcohol or over-the-counter medicines to help you sleep

Your polyclinic doctor can assess your sleep and refer you to a sleep specialist if needed. Some hospitals in Singapore offer sleep studies and CBT-I programmes.

Good sleep is within reach. Start with one or two changes from this guide, be consistent, and give it a few weeks to see results. Your body and mind will thank you.
