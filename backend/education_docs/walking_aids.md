# Walking Aids: Choosing and Using the Right One

Walking aids help older adults move safely, maintain independence, and stay active. Using a walking aid when you need one is a smart and practical decision. It is not a sign of giving up. Rather, it is a tool that allows you to continue doing the things you enjoy with greater confidence and safety. This guide covers the different types of walking aids, how to choose the right one, and how to use it properly.

## When Do You Need a Walking Aid?

You may benefit from a walking aid if you experience any of the following:
- Feeling unsteady or off-balance when walking
- Holding onto furniture or walls for support when moving around your home
- Having had one or more falls in the past year
- Feeling tired or breathless after walking short distances
- Experiencing pain in your legs, hips, or back when walking
- Walking noticeably slower than before
- Avoiding going out because you are afraid of falling
- Recovering from surgery, stroke, or other health events

A walking aid can also be useful even if you do not use it all the time. For example, you might use a walking stick when going to the wet market but not around your home.

If you think you might need a walking aid, talk to your doctor or physiotherapist. They can assess your mobility and recommend the most suitable type.

## Types of Walking Aids

### Walking Stick (Cane)

**Best for:** Mild balance problems, slight leg weakness on one side, or minor joint pain.

**Features:**
- Single point of contact with the ground
- Lightweight and easy to carry
- Available in various heights with adjustable options
- Some have ergonomic or cushioned handles for comfort

**When to choose a walking stick:**
- You need a little extra steadiness but can mostly walk on your own
- You have mild arthritis in one hip or knee
- You want a portable option for outdoor trips

**Types of walking sticks:**
- **Standard straight cane:** The most common type. Simple and lightweight.
- **Offset cane:** The handle is offset from the shaft, which distributes weight more evenly. Good for those with hand or wrist arthritis.
- **Folding cane:** Can be collapsed and stored in a bag when not in use. Convenient for travel.

### Quad Stick (Four-Point Cane)

**Best for:** Moderate balance problems or when you need more support than a regular cane.

**Features:**
- Four small feet at the base instead of one point
- Provides a wider base of support
- Can stand on its own when you let go
- Heavier than a standard cane

**When to choose a quad stick:**
- You need more stability than a regular cane provides
- You have moderate weakness on one side (such as after a stroke)
- You need to be able to let go of the cane briefly (for example, to open a door) without it falling over

### Walking Frame (Zimmer Frame)

**Best for:** Significant balance problems, bilateral (both sides) weakness, or after surgery.

**Features:**
- Four legs providing a very stable base
- Pick-up type (you lift and place it) or gliding type (front wheels with back rubber tips)
- Provides maximum support for standing and walking
- Can be used to lean on when resting

**When to choose a walking frame:**
- You need substantial support to stand and walk
- You are recovering from hip or knee surgery
- You have significant weakness in both legs
- You are at high risk of falls

**Types of walking frames:**
- **Standard pick-up frame:** You lift the entire frame and place it ahead of you with each step. Very stable but slower.
- **Front-wheeled frame:** Two wheels on the front legs allow you to push it forward. Easier to use than a pick-up frame but slightly less stable.

### Rollator (Wheeled Walker)

**Best for:** Those who can walk but tire easily, need to cover longer distances, or want to sit and rest periodically.

**Features:**
- Four wheels for easy pushing
- Hand brakes for stopping and safety
- Usually includes a built-in seat for resting
- Often has a basket or pouch for carrying items
- Available in various sizes including lightweight models

**When to choose a rollator:**
- You can walk but need balance support and somewhere to sit when tired
- You want to walk longer distances (to the market, park, or community club)
- You need to carry items but cannot hold a bag while using another aid
- You want the most natural walking pattern while using a walking aid

**Important safety note:** Rollators should not be used for heavy weight-bearing (leaning heavily on them for standing support), as they can roll away. If you need heavy support, a walking frame is safer.

## Choosing the Right Walking Aid

The best walking aid for you depends on several factors:

**Level of support needed:**
- Mild: Walking stick
- Moderate: Quad stick or front-wheeled walker
- Significant: Walking frame or rollator (with proper braking technique)

**Indoor vs outdoor use:**
- Indoor: Walking stick or walking frame (easier in tight HDB spaces)
- Outdoor: Rollator (covers longer distances, has a seat) or walking stick
- Both: Many people use different aids for different situations

**Hand and arm strength:**
- Walking frames require the ability to grip and some arm strength
- Rollators need enough hand strength to operate brakes
- Walking sticks require the least upper body strength

**Living environment:**
- In a small HDB flat, a standard walking frame or walking stick works well
- Rollators need more space to manoeuvre but are excellent outdoors
- Consider how the aid will work with lifts, MRT stations, and buses

## Proper Fitting and Use

An incorrectly fitted walking aid can increase your risk of falls rather than reduce it. Proper fitting is essential.

### Walking Stick Fitting

**Height:** Stand with your arm relaxed at your side. The handle should be at the level of your wrist crease. When gripping the handle, your elbow should be slightly bent (about 15 to 20 degrees).

**Which hand:** If you have a weaker or painful leg, hold the cane in the OPPOSITE hand. This distributes your weight more effectively. For example, if your left knee is painful, hold the cane in your right hand.

**Walking technique:**
1. Move the cane forward at the same time as your weaker or painful leg.
2. Press down on the cane as you step through with the other leg.
3. Keep a smooth, rhythmic pattern: cane and weak leg together, then strong leg.

**On stairs:**
- Going up: Strong leg first, then weak leg and cane together. Remember "good goes up."
- Going down: Cane first, then weak leg, then strong leg. Remember "bad goes down."
- Always use handrails when available for additional safety.

### Walking Frame Fitting

**Height:** Same as walking stick. Handles at wrist height, elbows slightly bent.

**Walking technique (pick-up frame):**
1. Lift the frame and place it one step ahead of you.
2. Step forward with your weaker leg first.
3. Then step forward with your stronger leg.
4. Do not drag the frame. Lift it cleanly each time.

**Walking technique (wheeled frame):**
1. Push the frame forward a comfortable distance.
2. Step into the frame, keeping your body centred between the back legs.
3. Do not reach too far forward, as this can cause you to lean and become unsteady.

### Rollator Use

**Brakes:** Practice using the brakes before you start walking. Squeeze both brakes to stop. Lock the brakes (push handles down) when sitting on the seat or standing still.

**Walking technique:**
1. Stand inside the rollator, not behind it.
2. Walk normally, pushing the rollator at a comfortable pace.
3. Keep the rollator close to your body. Do not let it get too far ahead.
4. When sitting on the built-in seat, ALWAYS lock the brakes first.

## Maintenance and Safety

- **Check rubber tips regularly.** Worn tips are slippery. Replace them when they become smooth. Rubber tips are inexpensive and available at medical supply shops across Singapore.
- **Check all screws and joints** monthly to ensure they are tight.
- **Keep your walking aid clean** and dry. Wipe down handles and frame regularly.
- **Replace any damaged parts** promptly. Do not use a bent or cracked walking aid.
- **Be extra careful on wet surfaces,** escalators, and uneven ground.
- **In hawker centres and coffee shops,** park your rollator where it will not block walkways or trip others.

## Where to Get Walking Aids in Singapore

- **Public hospitals and polyclinics:** Your physiotherapist or occupational therapist can recommend and fit the right walking aid. Subsidised options are available.
- **Seniors' Mobility and Enabling Fund (SMF):** Provides subsidies for walking aids and other assistive devices for eligible Singapore citizens and permanent residents. Apply through your healthcare provider.
- **Medical supply stores:** Available in many HDB estates and shopping centres. Brands like APEX and Bion are widely available. Basic walking canes cost $15 to $40, while rollators range from $80 to $300.
- **NTUC Health and Senior Activity Centres:** Some centres can help you access subsidised devices.

## Transitioning Between Aids

Your walking aid needs may change over time. This is completely normal.

- After surgery, you may start with a frame, progress to a quad cane, then to a single cane as you recover.
- If your strength improves through exercise, you may be able to move to a lighter aid.
- If you have a setback due to illness or injury, do not hesitate to use a more supportive aid temporarily.
- Your physiotherapist can guide these transitions safely.

## Adjusting Emotionally

It is natural to feel reluctant about using a walking aid. You may worry about how others see you or feel that it makes you look "old." These feelings are valid, but consider this:

- A walking aid allows you to go places you might otherwise avoid.
- It reduces your risk of falls and the serious consequences they bring.
- It gives your family peace of mind.
- Many active, independent older adults use walking aids every day.
- It is a tool, like reading glasses. You use it because it helps you do what you want to do.

The right walking aid, properly fitted and used, can make a real difference in your daily life. Talk to your healthcare team to find the best option for your needs. The goal is always to keep you moving safely and confidently.
