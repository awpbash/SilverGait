# Caregiver Self-Care: Looking After Yourself While Caring for Others

Caring for an elderly family member is one of the most meaningful things you can do, but it is also one of the most demanding. In Singapore, many caregivers are family members juggling caregiving with work, childcare, and other responsibilities. If you are a caregiver, this guide is for you. Taking care of yourself is not selfish. It is essential. You cannot pour from an empty cup.

## The Reality of Caregiving in Singapore

Singapore's ageing population means more families are providing care for elderly parents or spouses. According to the Agency for Integrated Care (AIC), there are over 200,000 informal caregivers in Singapore. Many provide care daily, often with limited help.

Caregiving can involve:
- Helping with bathing, dressing, toileting, and feeding
- Managing medications and medical appointments
- Preparing meals and managing the household
- Providing emotional support and companionship
- Coordinating with healthcare providers
- Managing finances and legal matters
- Supervising to prevent falls and wandering

The physical, emotional, and financial demands of caregiving can take a significant toll over time.

## Recognising Caregiver Burnout

Burnout does not happen overnight. It builds up gradually, and many caregivers do not recognise it until they are overwhelmed. Watch for these warning signs:

**Physical signs:**
- Constant tiredness, even after rest
- Frequent headaches, body aches, or illness
- Changes in appetite or weight
- Difficulty sleeping or sleeping too much
- Neglecting your own health (skipping check-ups, not taking your own medicines)

**Emotional signs:**
- Feeling irritable, anxious, or constantly worried
- Feeling sad, hopeless, or overwhelmed
- Loss of interest in activities you used to enjoy
- Feeling resentful towards the person you care for
- Feeling guilty about not doing enough
- Emotional numbness or feeling disconnected
- Crying more often than usual

**Behavioural signs:**
- Withdrawing from friends and social activities
- Using alcohol or medication to cope
- Snapping at family members or the person you care for
- Neglecting your own needs (meals, hygiene, exercise)
- Feeling that your life revolves entirely around caregiving

If you recognise several of these signs in yourself, please take action. You deserve support.

## Practical Self-Care Strategies

### Set Boundaries and Ask for Help

Many caregivers try to do everything themselves because they feel it is their duty. But caregiving is a team effort, and asking for help is a sign of wisdom, not weakness.

- **Divide tasks among family members.** Hold a family meeting to discuss who can help with what. Even small contributions like grocery shopping, accompanying to appointments, or providing meals on certain days make a difference.
- **Be specific when asking for help.** Instead of "I need help," say "Can you visit Mum on Saturday afternoon so I can rest?" or "Can you take Dad to the polyclinic on Tuesday?"
- **Set limits on what you can do.** It is okay to say "I cannot manage this on my own. We need additional support."
- **Consider hiring a helper** if your budget allows. Foreign domestic workers are a common source of support for caregiving families in Singapore.

### Make Time for Yourself

This is not optional. It is essential for sustaining your ability to care for someone else.

- **Schedule regular time for yourself** each week. Even 30 minutes of doing something you enjoy can recharge you.
- **Maintain your own health.** Keep your own medical appointments, take your medicines, eat regular meals, and try to get enough sleep.
- **Stay physically active.** Even a short walk each day can improve your mood and energy.
- **Stay socially connected.** Keep in touch with friends. Talk to someone about how you are feeling, not just about caregiving tasks.
- **Do something enjoyable** each day, however small: read a book, listen to music, tend your plants, or watch a favourite programme.

### Manage Stress

Caregiving stress is real and valid. Here are techniques that help:

- **Deep breathing:** Take 5 slow, deep breaths when you feel overwhelmed. Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, breathe out for 6.
- **Mindfulness:** Spend a few minutes each day focusing on the present moment rather than worrying about the future.
- **Journaling:** Writing down your thoughts and feelings can help you process difficult emotions.
- **Positive self-talk:** Replace "I should be doing more" with "I am doing my best, and that is enough."
- **Accept imperfection.** Not every day will go well. That is normal. Give yourself grace.

## Respite Care: Taking a Break

Respite care provides temporary relief for caregivers by arranging someone else to care for your loved one for a few hours, a day, or even longer.

**Types of respite care in Singapore:**

**Centre-based respite:**
- Day care centres provide supervision, meals, activities, and some therapy for older adults during the day. This allows caregivers to work, rest, or attend to other responsibilities.
- Some nursing homes offer short-term residential respite (a few days to a few weeks), useful when caregivers need to travel or recover from illness.

**Home-based respite:**
- Home care services can send a trained care worker to your home for a few hours.
- Some volunteers from befriending organisations can provide companionship for a few hours.

**How to access respite care:**
- Contact the AIC Silver Line at 1800-650-6060 for information on respite services near you.
- Your loved one's doctor or medical social worker can recommend suitable services.
- Ask at your nearest Senior Activity Centre or Family Service Centre.

**Subsidies and financial help:**
- Government subsidies are available for day care and home care services, based on means testing.
- The Home Caregiving Grant provides $200 per month for caregivers of those who need moderate to high levels of care (MediSave-eligible).
- Medifund provides a safety net for those who cannot afford care costs.
- The ElderFund supports low-income elderly who require long-term care services.

## Support Groups and Counselling

You are not alone. Connecting with other caregivers who understand what you are going through can be a lifeline.

**Caregiver support resources in Singapore:**

- **AIC Caregiver Support:** AIC runs caregiver support groups and training programmes. Call 1800-650-6060 or visit their website.
- **Caregiving Welfare Association:** Provides counselling, training, and support groups for caregivers.
- **TOUCH Caregivers Support:** Offers a helpline, support groups, and training for caregivers of older adults and persons with disabilities.
- **Family Service Centres (FSCs):** Located across Singapore, FSCs provide counselling, practical assistance, and referrals for families in need.
- **Caregiver Training Grant:** Covers up to $200 per year for caregivers to attend approved training courses on caregiving skills.

**Professional counselling:**
If you are feeling persistently overwhelmed, anxious, or depressed, please seek professional help:
- Talk to your own doctor at the polyclinic
- Call the Institute of Mental Health helpline: 6389 2222 (24 hours)
- Samaritans of Singapore (SOS): 1-767 (24 hours)
- Singapore Association for Mental Health: 1800-283-7019

## Planning for the Long Term

Caregiving often changes over time as the care recipient's needs increase. Planning ahead can reduce future stress.

- **Discuss preferences early.** Talk with your loved one about their wishes for care while they can still express them. Topics include preferred living arrangements, advance care planning, and lasting power of attorney.
- **Explore long-term care options.** Understand what is available (home care, day care, nursing homes) and the associated costs and subsidies so you are prepared if needs change.
- **Build a care team.** Do not rely on one person. Include family members, neighbours, community services, and professional caregivers.
- **Manage finances.** Understand MediShield Life, CareShield Life, and ElderShield coverage. A medical social worker can help you navigate the financial aspects of long-term care.

## A Message for Caregivers

Caregiving is hard. Some days will be rewarding, and some will be exhausting. Your dedication matters more than you know. But please remember:

- You have the right to take care of your own health and wellbeing.
- You have the right to ask for and accept help.
- You have the right to feel frustrated, sad, or tired without guilt.
- You have the right to maintain your own identity beyond being a caregiver.
- You have the right to set limits on what you can do.

You are doing important work. Make sure you are also taking care of the person in the mirror.
