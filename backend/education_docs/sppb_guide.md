# Understanding the SPPB: Your Physical Performance Assessment

The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) is a widely used assessment tool that measures how well your lower body is functioning. It is a reliable predictor of disability, falls, hospitalisation, and loss of independence. SilverGait uses the SPPB as one of its key assessment tools to track your physical performance and guide your exercise programme. This guide explains what the SPPB measures, what your scores mean, and how you can improve each component.

## What Is the SPPB?

The SPPB was developed by the National Institute on Aging in the United States and is now used worldwide, including in Singapore's healthcare system. It is a simple, practical test that takes about 10 minutes to complete.

The SPPB consists of three tests:
1. **Standing Balance Test** -- how well you can maintain your balance in different positions
2. **Gait Speed Test** -- how fast you walk a short distance at your normal pace
3. **Chair Stand Test** -- how quickly you can stand up from a chair without using your arms

Each test is scored from 0 to 4 points, giving a total SPPB score of 0 to 12 points.

## Understanding Your SPPB Score

| Total Score | What It Means |
|---|---|
| 10-12 | Good physical performance. You are functioning well. Continue your current exercise routine to maintain this level. |
| 7-9 | Moderate limitation. You may notice some difficulty with daily activities. Targeted exercise can improve your score. |
| 4-6 | Low performance. You likely have noticeable difficulty with mobility tasks. A structured exercise programme is strongly recommended. |
| 0-3 | Very low performance. You have significant physical limitations. Professional physiotherapy guidance is important. |

Research shows that even a 1-point improvement in SPPB score is clinically meaningful. It represents a real improvement in your ability to function in daily life.

## Component 1: Standing Balance Test

### What It Tests
This test measures your ability to maintain balance in three progressively challenging positions:

**Side-by-side stand:** Stand with your feet together, side by side, for 10 seconds.

**Semi-tandem stand:** Stand with the heel of one foot placed beside the big toe of the other foot (feet half-overlapping) for 10 seconds.

**Full tandem stand:** Stand with one foot directly in front of the other (heel of front foot touching toe of back foot) for 10 seconds.

### Scoring
- 0 points: Cannot hold side-by-side stand for 10 seconds
- 1 point: Can hold side-by-side stand for 10 seconds but not semi-tandem for 10 seconds
- 2 points: Can hold semi-tandem for 10 seconds but not full tandem for more than 2 seconds
- 3 points: Can hold full tandem for 3 to 9 seconds
- 4 points: Can hold full tandem for 10 seconds

### How to Improve Your Balance Score

**Daily practice (5 to 10 minutes):**
- Start with the position you can hold comfortably and gradually work towards harder positions.
- Practice near a wall or sturdy furniture for safety.
- Hold each position for as long as you can, rest, and repeat 3 times.

**Specific exercises:**
- **Heel-to-toe walking:** Walk in a straight line placing the heel of one foot directly in front of the toes of the other. Take 10 to 20 steps. Repeat 3 times.
- **Single-leg stands:** Stand on one leg for as long as you can (up to 30 seconds). Switch legs. Do 3 repetitions on each side.
- **Weight shifts:** Stand with feet hip-width apart. Shift your weight slowly to the left foot, then to the right foot. Do 10 shifts in each direction.
- **Tai Chi:** The slow, controlled movements of Tai Chi are excellent for improving standing balance. Many community clubs in Singapore offer free or subsidised Tai Chi classes for seniors.

**Progression:**
Once you can hold full tandem for 10 seconds easily, challenge yourself further by:
- Closing your eyes during balance holds (have support nearby)
- Standing on a folded towel for an uneven surface
- Turning your head side to side while in tandem position

## Component 2: Gait Speed Test

### What It Tests
You walk 4 metres (about 13 feet) at your usual walking pace. The time it takes is recorded. Gait speed is one of the most powerful predictors of health outcomes in older adults. It has been called the "sixth vital sign."

### Scoring
- 0 points: Unable to complete the walk
- 1 point: More than 8.70 seconds (less than 0.46 m/s)
- 2 points: 6.21 to 8.70 seconds (0.46 to 0.64 m/s)
- 3 points: 4.82 to 6.20 seconds (0.65 to 0.83 m/s)
- 4 points: Less than 4.82 seconds (more than 0.83 m/s)

A gait speed of 1.0 m/s or faster is generally considered a sign of good health and mobility.

### How to Improve Your Gait Speed

**Walking programme:**
- Walk regularly, aiming for at least 150 minutes per week.
- Start at a comfortable pace and gradually try to walk a little faster.
- Use a timer or tracking app to monitor your pace.
- Walk in safe, flat areas. Parks, covered walkways, and HDB void decks are good options in Singapore.

**Strengthening exercises (these directly improve walking speed):**
- **Calf raises:** Rise up on your toes and lower slowly. Do 10 to 15 repetitions, 2 to 3 sets.
- **Mini squats:** Stand behind a chair, bend your knees, and lower slightly, then stand back up. Do 10 repetitions, 2 to 3 sets.
- **Step-ups:** Step up onto a low step (like a stair) with one foot, bring the other foot up, then step back down. Alternate legs. Do 10 on each side.
- **Toe raises:** Rock back onto your heels, lifting your toes off the ground. This strengthens shin muscles and helps prevent tripping. Do 10 to 15 repetitions.

**Stride and posture:**
- Focus on taking slightly longer steps rather than just faster ones.
- Walk tall with your head up and shoulders back. Looking down at the ground shortens your stride.
- Swing your arms naturally. This helps with balance and rhythm.
- Wear supportive, well-fitting shoes with good grip.

## Component 3: Chair Stand Test

### What It Tests
You sit in a standard-height chair with your arms crossed over your chest. You stand up and sit back down 5 times as quickly as you can. The time is recorded. This test measures leg strength and power, which are essential for daily activities like getting up from a chair, climbing stairs, and recovering from a stumble.

### Scoring
- 0 points: Unable to complete 5 stands, or takes more than 60 seconds
- 1 point: 16.70 seconds or more
- 2 points: 13.70 to 16.69 seconds
- 3 points: 11.20 to 13.69 seconds
- 4 points: 11.19 seconds or less

### How to Improve Your Chair Stand Score

**Practice the movement:**
- Start by practicing sit-to-stand from a higher chair or with a cushion on the seat to make it easier.
- Use your arms initially if needed, then gradually transition to standing without hands.
- Focus on leaning forward (nose over toes) before standing. This shifts your weight forward and makes standing easier.
- Do 3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions, resting between sets.

**Strengthening exercises:**
- **Wall squats:** Stand with your back against a wall. Slide down until your knees are bent to about 45 degrees. Hold for 10 to 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times. Progress by going lower or holding longer.
- **Leg extensions:** Sit in a chair. Straighten one leg out in front, hold for 5 seconds, lower slowly. Do 10 on each leg. Add ankle weights as you get stronger.
- **Step-ups:** Step up onto a low stair, alternating legs. Do 10 on each side.
- **Squats with chair:** Stand in front of a chair, lower yourself to almost sitting, then stand back up without fully sitting. Do 10 repetitions.

**Progression:**
- Reduce the height of the chair as you get stronger.
- Add a small weight to hold (like a water bottle) for extra resistance.
- Try to do the 5 stands faster each week.

## Tracking Your Progress

Monitoring your SPPB score over time helps you see your improvement and stay motivated.

- SilverGait tracks your SPPB score automatically after each video assessment.
- Aim to reassess every 2 to 4 weeks to track progress.
- A 1-point improvement is clinically meaningful and worth celebrating.
- Even maintaining your score over time is a positive outcome, as it means you are resisting age-related decline.
- Focus on the overall trend rather than any single assessment.

## Tips for Your SPPB Assessment

- Wear comfortable, supportive shoes (not slippers or socks).
- Make sure you are on a flat, non-slippery surface.
- Warm up with a few minutes of gentle movement beforehand.
- Give your best effort, but do not push through pain.
- If you feel dizzy or unsteady at any point, stop and rest.

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor or physiotherapist if:
- Your score drops by 2 or more points between assessments
- You experience a fall or near-fall
- You have new pain during any of the tests
- You feel unsafe doing the exercises on your own
- You are not seeing improvement after 4 to 6 weeks of regular exercise

In Singapore, you can access physiotherapy through polyclinic referrals, community rehabilitation programmes coordinated by AIC, or senior activity centres that offer group exercise sessions. The Community Health Posts also provide functional screening that includes SPPB-type assessments.

Your SPPB score is a snapshot of your current physical function. With consistent exercise, good nutrition, and the right support, you have the power to improve it. Every point matters.
