# Physiotherapy Exercises for Older Adults

Regular exercise is one of the most effective ways to prevent falls, maintain independence, and reverse early frailty. This guide covers evidence-based exercise programmes recommended by physiotherapists, including the internationally recognised Otago Exercise Programme. All exercises can be adapted to your fitness level and done at home with minimal equipment.

## Why Exercise Matters for Older Adults

Physical activity provides wide-ranging benefits for older adults:
- **Stronger muscles:** Resistance exercises slow down age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia) and can rebuild lost strength.
- **Better balance:** Balance training reduces fall risk by up to 50 percent.
- **Healthier bones:** Weight-bearing exercises help maintain bone density and reduce fracture risk.
- **Improved mood:** Exercise releases chemicals in the brain that improve mood and reduce anxiety.
- **Better sleep:** Regular physical activity helps you fall asleep more easily and sleep more deeply.
- **Heart health:** Walking and other aerobic activities keep your heart and blood vessels healthy.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that adults aged 65 and above do at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, along with muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days per week.

## The Otago Exercise Programme

The Otago Exercise Programme (OEP) is a well-researched physiotherapy programme developed specifically for older adults. Studies have shown it reduces falls by up to 35 percent. It consists of leg strengthening exercises and balance exercises, combined with a walking plan.

**Key principles of the Otago programme:**
- Exercises are done 3 times per week at home.
- Walking is done at least 2 times per week.
- The programme is progressive, meaning exercises get gradually harder over time.
- It takes about 30 minutes per session.

### Otago Strengthening Exercises

These exercises target the key muscle groups needed for stability and mobility. Use ankle weights when you are ready to progress (start with 0.5 to 1 kg and increase gradually).

**1. Knee Bends (Mini Squats):**
Stand behind a chair, holding the backrest. Bend your knees slowly, keeping your back straight and your heels on the ground. Go down as far as comfortable, then stand back up. Repeat 10 times. Progress by going deeper or adding ankle weights.

**2. Backwards Leg Raises:**
Stand behind a chair, holding the backrest. Slowly lift one leg straight behind you, keeping your knee straight. Hold for a moment, then lower slowly. Repeat 10 times on each leg.

**3. Sideways Leg Raises:**
Stand behind a chair, holding the backrest. Lift one leg out to the side, keeping your body upright. Hold briefly, then lower. Repeat 10 times on each side.

**4. Calf Raises:**
Stand behind a chair. Rise up on your toes as high as you can. Hold for 3 seconds, then lower slowly. Repeat 10 times. Progress by doing the exercise on one leg at a time.

**5. Toe Raises:**
Stand behind a chair. Rock back onto your heels, lifting your toes off the ground. Hold for 3 seconds, then lower. Repeat 10 times. This strengthens the muscles at the front of your shin, which help prevent tripping.

### Otago Balance Exercises

**1. Knee Bends with Eyes Closed:**
Once you can do mini squats comfortably, try doing them with your eyes closed (keep a hand near the chair for safety). This challenges your balance further.

**2. Walking Backwards:**
Walk backwards for 10 steps in a clear space, near a wall for safety. This improves coordination and balance.

**3. Figure of Eight Walking:**
Walk in a figure of eight pattern around two objects placed about 2 metres apart. This challenges turning balance.

**4. Tandem Stand:**
Stand with one foot directly in front of the other (heel to toe). Hold for 10 to 30 seconds. Switch feet.

**5. One Leg Stand:**
Stand on one leg for up to 30 seconds. Stay near a chair. Switch legs.

## Chair-Based Exercises

If you have difficulty standing for extended periods or feel very unsteady, chair-based exercises are an excellent starting point. Use a sturdy chair without wheels.

**Seated Marching:**
Sit tall in your chair. Lift one knee up towards your chest, then lower. Alternate legs as if marching. Continue for 1 minute. This strengthens hip flexors and improves circulation.

**Seated Leg Extensions:**
Sit with your back supported. Straighten one leg out in front of you, hold for 3 seconds, then lower slowly. Repeat 10 times on each leg. This strengthens your quadriceps, which are critical for standing and walking.

**Seated Heel and Toe Raises:**
Place your feet flat on the floor. Lift your heels off the ground, pressing through your toes. Then lower your heels and lift your toes. Alternate between the two for 20 repetitions.

**Seated Side Bends:**
Sit upright with your arms at your sides. Slowly bend to one side, reaching your hand down towards the floor. Return to centre and bend to the other side. Repeat 10 times on each side. This improves trunk flexibility.

**Arm Raises with Water Bottles:**
Hold a water bottle (500ml) in each hand. Start with your arms at your sides. Lift both arms out to the sides and up to shoulder height. Hold for 2 seconds, then lower slowly. Repeat 10 times. This maintains upper body strength needed for daily tasks.

## Progressing Your Exercises

Progression is key to continued improvement. Here is a general guide:

**Weeks 1-2:** Learn the exercises with support. Focus on doing each movement correctly. Do 5 to 8 repetitions.

**Weeks 3-4:** Increase to 10 repetitions. Reduce how much you hold onto the chair. Try to just rest your fingertips on it.

**Weeks 5-8:** Add ankle weights (start with 0.5 kg). Try balance exercises with minimal support. Increase walking distance.

**Weeks 9-12:** Increase ankle weights to 1 to 2 kg. Try standing exercises with eyes closed (near a support). Walk for 30 minutes.

**Ongoing:** Continue 3 times per week. Increase challenge whenever exercises feel too easy. Vary your routine to keep it interesting.

## Safety Tips

- Warm up with gentle marching or arm circles for 2 to 3 minutes before starting.
- Breathe normally throughout. Do not hold your breath during exercises.
- Stop if you feel sharp pain, severe dizziness, or chest discomfort.
- Some muscle soreness the next day is normal when starting a new programme. Severe or lasting pain is not.
- Exercise at the same time each day to build a routine.
- Keep a record of your exercises to track progress.

## When to See a Physiotherapist

Consider getting professional guidance if:
- You have had a recent fall or surgery.
- You have a condition like stroke, Parkinson's disease, or severe arthritis that affects your movement.
- You are unsure whether an exercise is safe for you.
- You have not exercised for a long time and want a personalised starting plan.

In Singapore, you can access physiotherapy through:
- **Polyclinic referral:** Your polyclinic doctor can refer you to physiotherapy at a restructured hospital or community health centre.
- **Community Rehabilitation Programme:** AIC provides community-based rehabilitation services closer to home.
- **Senior Activity Centres:** Some centres have physiotherapists who run group exercise sessions.
- **Private physiotherapy clinics:** No referral needed, but costs are higher.

The most important thing is to start. Even gentle exercises done consistently will produce results. Choose the level that matches your current ability and build from there.
