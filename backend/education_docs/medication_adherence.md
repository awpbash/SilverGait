# Managing Your Medications Safely

As we age, it is common to take multiple medications for different health conditions. In Singapore, many older adults take five or more medicines daily. Managing these medications properly is essential for your health and safety. This guide covers practical strategies for taking your medicines correctly and working with your healthcare team.

## Why Medication Management Matters

Taking the right medicine, at the right dose, at the right time sounds simple, but it can be challenging when you have several medications. Poor medication management can lead to:
- **Side effects** that reduce your quality of life, such as dizziness, drowsiness, or stomach upset
- **Drug interactions** where one medicine affects how another works
- **Worsening of your condition** if you miss doses or stop too early
- **Falls** caused by medicines that affect balance, blood pressure, or alertness
- **Hospitalisations** that could have been prevented

Studies show that medication-related problems are one of the leading causes of hospital admissions among older adults. Many of these can be avoided with good medication management.

## Understanding Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy means taking five or more regular medications. While each individual medicine may be needed, having many medicines together increases the risk of side effects and interactions.

**Common concerns with polypharmacy:**
- Some combinations of medicines increase fall risk. Blood pressure pills, sleeping tablets, antihistamines, and strong painkillers can all cause dizziness or drowsiness.
- Medicines may have overlapping effects. For example, two different blood pressure medications may lower your pressure too much.
- More medicines means more chances of confusion about doses, timing, and purpose.
- The cost of multiple medications can add up, even with subsidies.

**What you can do:**
- Bring all your medicines (including traditional medicines, supplements, and over-the-counter products) to every doctor visit. Use a plastic bag to carry them all.
- Ask your doctor at least once a year: "Do I still need all of these medicines?"
- Never stop a medicine without talking to your doctor first.
- If a new medicine is added, ask whether any existing ones can be stopped.

## Strategies for Taking Medicines Correctly

### Use a Pill Organiser

A weekly pill organiser (also called a pill box or dosette box) is one of the simplest and most effective tools for medication management. It has separate compartments for each day of the week and different times of day (morning, afternoon, evening, night).

**Tips for using a pill organiser:**
- Fill it at the same time each week, such as every Sunday evening.
- Fill it in a well-lit area with no distractions.
- Double-check each compartment against your medicine list before closing.
- Keep it in a visible, consistent place, such as next to your kettle or on the dining table.
- Pharmacists at polyclinics and hospitals can help you set up your pill organiser.

### Set Reminders

- Set alarms on your phone or clock for each medication time.
- Link medicine-taking to daily routines, such as after breakfast or before brushing your teeth at night.
- Ask a family member to remind you, especially for important medicines like blood thinners or insulin.
- Some pharmacies offer medication reminder apps and services.

### Keep a Medicine List

Maintain an updated list of all your medicines. Include:
- Medicine name
- Dose (e.g., 5mg, 500mg)
- How often to take it
- What it is for
- Any special instructions (e.g., take with food, avoid grapefruit)

Keep a copy in your wallet, give one to your caregiver, and bring it to all medical appointments. In Singapore, the National Electronic Health Record (NEHR) stores your prescription history, but keeping your own list is still valuable.

### Understand Each Medicine

For every medicine you take, you should know:
- **What is it for?** For example, "This is for my blood pressure."
- **When do I take it?** Morning, evening, with food, or on an empty stomach.
- **What are common side effects?** Knowing what to expect helps you report problems early.
- **What should I avoid?** Some medicines interact with certain foods (like grapefruit with some blood pressure pills) or other medicines.

If you are unsure about any of these, ask your doctor or pharmacist. They are there to help.

## The Role of Your Pharmacist

Pharmacists are medication experts and an important resource for older adults. In Singapore, you can access pharmacist services at:
- **Polyclinics:** Pharmacists will explain your medicines when you collect them. Do not hesitate to ask questions.
- **Restructured hospitals:** Hospital pharmacists conduct medication reconciliation and can advise on complex medication regimens.
- **Community pharmacies:** Pharmacists at Guardian, Watsons, and Unity can answer questions about over-the-counter medicines and check for interactions with your prescribed medicines.
- **Medication Therapy Management (MTM):** Some polyclinics offer MTM services where a pharmacist reviews all your medicines in detail and works with your doctor to optimise your medications.

**Questions to ask your pharmacist:**
- Can I take this new medicine with my existing ones?
- Is there a cheaper alternative that works the same way?
- What should I do if I miss a dose?
- Should I take this with food or on an empty stomach?
- Are there any side effects I should watch for?

## Medicines That Increase Fall Risk

Some medicines are particularly important to be aware of if you are at risk of falls:

- **Sleeping pills and sedatives:** Can cause drowsiness, dizziness, and confusion. These effects can linger into the next day.
- **Blood pressure medications:** Can cause dizziness when you stand up (postural hypotension). Stand up slowly and hold onto something stable.
- **Antihistamines:** Older types (like chlorpheniramine) cause drowsiness. Newer types (like cetirizine) are less sedating.
- **Strong painkillers (opioids):** Can cause dizziness, drowsiness, and confusion.
- **Diabetes medications:** Can cause low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), leading to dizziness, confusion, and falls.
- **Antidepressants and antipsychotics:** Some types can affect balance and alertness.

If you are taking any of these and experiencing falls or near-falls, talk to your doctor. Do not stop any medicine without medical advice.

## Special Considerations

**If you have difficulty swallowing tablets:**
- Ask your pharmacist if the medicine comes in a liquid, dissolvable, or patch form.
- Some tablets can be crushed and mixed with food, but not all. Check with your pharmacist first, as crushing some medicines can be dangerous.

**If you use traditional or herbal medicines:**
- Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about any traditional medicines, herbal supplements, or health products you take.
- Some herbal products interact with prescribed medicines. For example, ginkgo and ginseng can interact with blood thinners.

**If cost is a concern:**
- Ask about generic alternatives, which contain the same active ingredient at lower cost.
- The Community Health Assist Scheme (CHAS) provides subsidised medicines at participating clinics.
- Pioneer and Merdeka Generation cardholders receive additional subsidies.
- Medifund provides a safety net for patients who cannot afford their medical bills.

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor or pharmacist if:
- You experience new symptoms after starting a medicine
- You are confused about how to take your medicines
- You are struggling to afford your medicines
- You want to stop or change a medicine
- You have trouble swallowing your medicines

Good medication management is a team effort involving you, your family, your doctor, and your pharmacist. Do not be afraid to ask questions. Understanding your medicines helps you stay safe and get the most benefit from them.
